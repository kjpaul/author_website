## Orbital rings? Mass drivers? Space elevators? This book has the numbers.

I do not sugar-coat technology, and this is an engineering book, not a sales pitch. Orbital rings, mass drivers and space elevators are easy to talk about; the numbers are the hard part. Every number in this book comes from a derivation shown in it or in Volumes II and III, or from the open-source simulation code written for the series, and every one of them can be checked. The code is on the [Code](/code/) page, the references for every chapter are on this site, and errors go on the [errata page](/errata/).

## What an orbital ring is

An orbital ring is a cable in orbit around the Earth, moving faster than orbital velocity, with a stationary casing riding on it. Anchor lines tie the casing to the ground. Trolley cars climb them, a mass driver on the ring throws spacecraft to the Moon, Mars and beyond, and space elevators bring people home. No rockets, no fuel, no countdown. It is mass transit to space, with more capacity than ten thousand rocket launches a year.

## What this volume does

Volume I is the complete design review, and the volume to read first. It takes the ring as a whole, at one altitude, with one cable, one bearing, one set of motors and one power system, and asks whether it all works together. The answer is mostly yes, with the caveats stated, and the open questions are listed for the team that eventually builds the ring. Volumes II and III carry the derivations behind the numbers: the first principles, and the launch technologies.

## Inside this volume

- Why the ring is at 700 km, and what a cable break at 250 km would do to the climate
- The carbon nanotube cable: 25 GPa, 846 GN of tension, and the two assumptions the whole design rests on
- Superconducting levitation, the linear motors that deploy the ring, and the power to run them
- The mass driver, the anchor lines, the suspended solar array and the space elevators
- Debris, damage and repair, and the load the casing can carry
- Rings at the Moon and Mars, and the missions a ring launches

Nineteen chapters, an appendix on the simulation code, a glossary and an index; 450 pages.
